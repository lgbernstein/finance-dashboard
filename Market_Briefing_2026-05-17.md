## What Just Happened — The Bond Yield Story

You mentioned the 5.12% figure. Here is the precise picture:

The 30-year US Treasury bond yield hit 5.12% this week. The 10-year Treasury yield hit 4.59%, its highest level since February 2025. Both moved sharply higher in a short period. That matters, and here is why in plain language.

**What is a Treasury bond yield, and why should you care?**

When the US government needs money, it borrows it by selling bonds. You hand the government $1,000 today, and they promise to pay you back in 10 or 30 years, plus regular interest payments along the way. The "yield" is simply the annual interest rate that deal works out to.

Here is the key insight: bond yields and bond prices move in opposite directions. When yields go up, the value of bonds you already own goes down. Think of it like this — if new bonds are now paying 5%, nobody wants to pay full price for your old bond that only pays 3%. So your old bond drops in value until its effective return matches the new 5% rate.

If you own bonds in your retirement account, the value of those bonds has gone down this week.

## Why Are Yields Rising Right Now?

Three things are driving this, all connected:

**1. Inflation is coming back**

The most recent government reports on prices (called CPI and PPI) showed that energy prices — oil, gas, heating — are pushing costs higher across the economy. When inflation rises, bond investors demand higher interest rates to compensate. They are saying: "If prices are going up 4-5% per year, I need to earn more than 4% on my bonds or I am losing ground in real terms."

**2. The Strait of Hormuz is closed**

This is the narrow waterway between Iran and Oman through which roughly 20% of the world's oil passes. It is currently closed due to the conflict in the Middle East. When oil cannot flow freely, oil prices rise. Higher oil prices push up the cost of almost everything — shipping, manufacturing, food, plastics. This is feeding directly into the inflation numbers above.

**3. The Federal Reserve may raise rates, not cut them**

Six months ago, most investors expected the Fed to cut interest rates in 2026 — meaning cheaper borrowing for everyone. That expectation has now completely reversed. Investors are now pricing in a potential rate hike before the end of 2026. The Fed raises rates to fight inflation. Higher Fed rates pull all other interest rates up with them.

## What This Means for Your Portfolio

**Your bonds and bond funds:** Values have dropped this week. This is a paper loss — it only becomes a real loss if you sell. If your bonds are held to maturity inside a retirement account, you will still receive the full face value when they mature. The question is whether you need that money before maturity.

**Your stocks:** Rising yields are bad for stocks in the short term for two reasons. First, bonds at 5%+ become an attractive alternative to stocks — investors shift money from risky stocks into safer bonds. Second, companies borrow money to grow. Higher rates mean higher borrowing costs, which squeezes profits. US equity futures pointed lower this week. Asia and European markets also fell.

**Your retirement accounts overall:** If your retirement accounts hold a mix of stocks and bonds (which most do), this week likely showed losses in both. This is not unusual during a rising-rate, rising-inflation environment. The critical question for your advisor: how close are you to needing this money, and is your current mix appropriate for this environment?

**A note on why this week is unusual:** In normal markets, when stocks fall, bonds rise. Investors sell stocks and park money in bonds as a "safe haven," which pushes bond prices up. This week, both fell at the same time. That happens during inflation scares — investors don't trust bonds either because inflation erodes their fixed payments. It is the hardest environment for a traditional retirement portfolio to navigate, and it is exactly why your asset allocation deserves a fresh look right now.

## The Bigger Picture — "Second Wave of Inflation"

Some economists are now using the phrase "second wave of inflation." The first wave hit in 2021-2023 and was driven by COVID supply chain disruptions and stimulus spending. The Fed raised rates aggressively to fight it, and inflation came down.

This second wave appears driven by geopolitical disruption — specifically the energy supply shock from the Middle East conflict. The danger is that energy inflation bleeds into "core" inflation (everything except food and energy), which is harder to fix and takes longer to reverse.

The uncomfortable scenario: the Fed may have to raise rates again even as the economy slows. When that happens — higher rates AND slower growth — it is called stagflation. It is rare and difficult to navigate. It is not a certainty, but it is now being discussed seriously by market analysts.

## Questions to Bring to Your Financial Advisor

1. Given that both stocks and bonds are down at the same time, is our current asset allocation still appropriate, or should we consider shifting?

   What this means: "Asset allocation" is simply how your money is divided between stocks and bonds (and sometimes cash or other assets). You will often see it expressed as two numbers — for example 70/30, 60/40, or 55/45. The first number is the percentage in stocks. The second is the percentage in bonds.

   A 70/30 portfolio is more aggressive. You own more stocks, which means more growth potential in good times, but bigger swings when markets fall. This is typically appropriate when you have a long time horizon — 15 or more years before you need the money — because you have time to ride out downturns.

   A 60/40 portfolio is the classic "balanced" allocation. For decades it was the standard recommendation for retirement investors. The idea was that when stocks fell, bonds would rise and cushion the blow. This week is a reminder that the 60/40 strategy has a weakness: it fails when stocks and bonds fall together, which happens during inflation-driven crises.

   A 55/45 or 50/50 portfolio is more conservative. You sacrifice some long-term growth in exchange for more stability. This makes sense as you get closer to retirement and have less time to recover from a big loss.

   For your specific situation: Larry is 68 and retired, Maria is 61 and still working. This changes the calculus in two ways. First, Larry is in the distribution phase — drawing from accounts, not adding to them. A big loss now is harder to recover from than a loss at age 45, because withdrawals continue regardless of what markets do. This argues for a more conservative allocation than 70/30. Second, Maria's income provides a real cushion. You are not forced to sell retirement assets to cover living expenses right now, which means you have time to let a downturn recover. This argues against going so conservative that you sacrifice all long-term growth — Maria potentially has 25+ years of investing horizon ahead.

   A reasonable starting point for discussion: somewhere between 55/45 and 60/40, with the bond side weighted toward shorter durations and inflation-protected bonds (TIPS) given the current environment. But your advisor knows your full picture. This week is a good prompt to have that specific conversation.

   This week is also a good real-world test of one other thing: how did you feel watching the portfolio drop? If the answer is "very anxious," that is useful data. Your emotional response to volatility is a legitimate input into allocation decisions.

2. What is the duration of the bonds in our portfolio? (Longer duration bonds are hurt more by rising rates than short-duration bonds.)

   What this means: "Duration" measures how sensitive a bond is to interest rate changes. A bond that matures in 30 years is far more affected by a rate rise than one that matures in 2 years. If you own a long-duration bond fund inside your retirement account, it likely took a bigger hit this week than a short-duration fund. Ask your advisor specifically whether your bond holdings are short, intermediate, or long duration — and whether now is the time to shorten it.

3. Are any of our bond holdings in short-term Treasuries or money market funds that are actually benefiting from higher rates right now?

   What this means: Not all fixed income suffers in a rising-rate environment. Short-term Treasury bills (often called T-bills) and money market funds reset their interest rates quickly, so they start paying you more when rates rise. If your portfolio has none of these, you may be missing a pocket of safety that is currently paying over 5% with almost no risk.

4. If stagflation becomes the base case, what assets tend to hold value in that environment — commodities, TIPS (inflation-protected bonds), real assets?

   What this means: TIPS stands for Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities. They are government bonds where the face value automatically adjusts upward with inflation, so your purchasing power is protected. Commodities (gold, oil, agricultural goods) and real estate have historically held value during inflationary periods. None of these are a guarantee, but they behave differently from standard stocks and bonds, which is why they become worth discussing when inflation is the main concern.

5. How much cash buffer do we have, and does it need to increase if markets stay volatile?

   What this means: Having some cash or cash-equivalent holdings (money market funds, short-term CDs, T-bills) serves two purposes. First, it means you do not have to sell stocks or bonds at a loss if you need money unexpectedly. Second, it gives you "dry powder" — money available to buy assets at lower prices if markets fall further. Most advisors suggest keeping 6-12 months of living expenses in accessible cash regardless of market conditions.

   For your situation specifically: because Larry is retired and drawing income from the portfolio, some advisors recommend a "bucket strategy" — keeping 1-2 years of expenses in cash or near-cash so that short-term market swings never force a sale at the wrong time. Maria's income reduces this pressure, but it is still worth confirming the cash buffer is adequate.

6. Are we planning for Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs)?

   What this means: At age 73 — roughly five years away for Larry — the IRS requires you to withdraw a minimum amount from traditional IRAs and 401k accounts each year and pay income tax on it. You cannot skip this or delay it. If markets are down when RMDs begin, you are forced to sell assets at lower prices to meet the requirement. Planning now — while Maria is still earning and you are not forced to touch retirement accounts — gives you options. One strategy some people use is converting portions of traditional IRA money to Roth IRA now (called a Roth conversion), paying tax at today's rates to avoid forced taxable withdrawals later. Ask your advisor whether this makes sense given your current income and tax bracket.
