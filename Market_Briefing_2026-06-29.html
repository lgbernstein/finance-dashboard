Market Briefing — June 29, 2026

For Larry and Maria  |  Focus: Stocks, Bonds, Retirement Accounts  |  Prepared by Claude
Bond yields fell to a seven-week low this week as inflation data came in as expected, easing fears of Fed rate hikes. But fresh US-Iran strikes on Monday pushed oil back up. Calmer inflation pulled interest rates down and let stocks push back toward record highs. The renewed Middle East tension is the wildcard that could undo that progress.

What Happened This Week

This was a calmer week, and most of the news leaned in your favor. The government released its PCE inflation report, the Federal Reserve's preferred measure of how fast prices are rising, and it came in roughly as forecasters expected. That mattered because it eased worries that the Fed would have to raise interest rates again this year. Investors had been bracing for a hot number. They did not get one.

The result showed up first in the bond market. The yield on the 10-year Treasury note fell to about 4.38%, down 7 basis points on the week, and dipped to its lowest level in seven weeks on Thursday. A Treasury note is a loan you make to the US government, and the yield is the yearly interest it pays. When investors expect the Fed to hold rates steady or lower them, yields fall. This week they fell, which signals the market grew a little more relaxed about inflation.

Stocks took that as a green light. On Monday the Nasdaq rose about 1%, the S&P 500 added 0.7%, and the Dow climbed roughly 150 points to test a record above 52,000. The S&P 500 now sits within about 3% of its all-time high, even after some bumpy trading in technology stocks last week. The one sour note came from oil. Crude prices climbed again on Monday after the United States and Iran traded fresh strikes, with US West Texas crude rising 2.4% to about $70.85 a barrel. Note that markets close early this week and stay shut on Friday, July 3, for the Independence Day holiday.

What is the PCE inflation report? PCE stands for Personal Consumption Expenditures. It is the price gauge the Federal Reserve watches most closely when it decides whether to move interest rates. It tracks what Americans actually spend across goods and services. When it comes in as expected, the Fed feels less pressure to act, which is what happened this week.
What is a basis point? A basis point is one hundredth of one percent. So 7 basis points equals 0.07%. The 10-year yield falling 7 basis points means it dropped from about 4.45% to 4.38%. Small moves like this matter because they ripple into mortgage rates, bond prices, and the cost of borrowing across the economy.

What This Means for Your Portfolio

Your stocks A good week. The Dow tested a record high and the broad market climbed back toward its peak. The stock portion of your retirement accounts gained value. The caution flag is that prices sit near record levels and technology shares have been jumpy, so a pullback would not be a surprise if the Fed turns more hawkish or oil keeps rising.
Your bonds and bond funds Also a good week. Bond prices and bond yields move in opposite directions. When yields fall, the bonds you already own become more valuable. Yields fell this week, so your existing bond holdings ticked up in value. The bigger picture stays favorable too: yields near 4.4% mean any new bonds, CDs, or money market funds you buy now pay solid interest, which helps your income in retirement.
Your retirement accounts overall Holding steady to slightly higher. Both stocks and bonds gained this week, so your total balance likely rose. There is no action forced on you. The main thing to keep an eye on is whether renewed Middle East tension pushes oil and inflation back up, which would reverse some of this week's gains.

Your situation gives you room to stay calm through weeks like this. Larry, you are 68 and retired, so you are in the distribution phase, meaning you draw money from your accounts rather than add to them. Maria, you are 61 and still working, and your paycheck covers household costs. That means you are not a forced seller. You never have to sell stocks at a bad price to pay bills. Your required minimum distributions, the withdrawals the government makes you take from retirement accounts, do not begin until Larry turns 73, about five years out. That gap is valuable. It lets you ride out short-term swings and treat any market dip as a chance rather than a threat.

The Bigger Picture

The economy is in a late-cycle stretch: growth is solid but inflation is running hot. Jobs are still being added, unemployment sits at 4.3%, and consumer spending holds up. At the same time, inflation reached 4.2% over the past year in the May report, more than double the Fed's 2% goal, almost entirely because of the oil price spike from the US-Iran war earlier this year. The new Fed Chair, Kevin Warsh, has taken a tougher stance and signaled the Fed could raise rates rather than cut them. That is the central tension in markets right now.

This week the optimistic side won. In-line inflation data and falling oil earlier in the week let yields drop and stocks rise. But the renewed US-Iran strikes on Monday are a reminder that the whole story still hinges on oil. As long as energy prices stay contained, inflation should cool over the summer and the Fed can hold steady. If the Middle East flares up again, oil climbs, inflation stays stuck, and the path points toward a rate hike.

Renewed US-Iran strikes → oil supply fears return, crude climbs back toward $73 → energy keeps inflation elevated → Fed leans toward a rate hike → bond yields and mortgage rates rise, stock prices come under pressure

What to Watch Over the Next Two Weeks

  • The June jobs report, due Thursday, July 2 (moved up because of the holiday). A reading near the recent pace with unemployment around 4.3% signals a healthy labor market. A sharp miss below 100,000 jobs would ease rate-hike pressure.
  • The June CPI inflation report, due July 14. This is the most important number on the calendar. A reading near 3% would strongly reduce the case for a Fed hike. A reading stuck near 4% confirms the Fed's tougher stance.
  • The US-Iran situation and oil prices. Each headline moves oil, and oil drives inflation. If strikes escalate, crude jumps and inflation fears return. If tensions cool, oil stays in the $70s and the pressure on the Fed fades.
  • The 10-year Treasury yield, now about 4.38%. Above 4.75% signals markets expect rate hikes. Below 4.25% signals confidence that inflation is cooling.
  • The stock market holiday. US markets close early and stay shut Friday, July 3, so trading volume will be light and moves can be exaggerated.

Questions to Bring to Your Financial Advisor

1 The Fed may raise rates instead of cutting them. Are our bond holdings positioned for that?

When interest rates rise, the value of existing bonds falls, and longer-term bonds fall the most. This sensitivity is called duration. With the Fed now hinting at hikes, it is worth asking whether your bond funds lean long or short. Shorter-term bonds and CDs lose less value if rates climb and still pay attractive interest at today's levels.

2 With Maria still working, is this a good window for Roth conversions before RMDs begin?

A Roth conversion moves money from a traditional retirement account to a Roth account, where future growth and withdrawals are tax-free. You pay tax now on the amount converted. Maria's income can help cover that tax bill, and the roughly five years before Larry's required withdrawals start at 73 are an ideal window. Any market dip makes conversions cheaper because you move shares at lower values. Ask about the right annual amount.

3 Stocks are near record highs with jumpy tech shares. Are we comfortable with our stock allocation?

Markets are within about 3% of all-time highs, and technology valuations are elevated. This is a fair moment to confirm your mix of stocks and bonds still matches your comfort with risk, especially since you are drawing from these accounts. The goal is not to time the market but to make sure a pullback would not force any uncomfortable decisions.

4 Should we lock in today's higher yields on cash with CDs or Treasuries?

Short-term Treasuries, CDs, and money market funds pay solid interest right now because the Fed has kept rates elevated. If you hold cash you will not need for a while, locking in a yield through a CD or short Treasury can secure that income before any future Fed cut lowers it. Ask how much of your cash makes sense to commit and for how long.
