Market Briefing — July 13, 2026

For Larry and Maria  |  Focus: Stocks, Bonds, Retirement Accounts  |  Prepared by Claude
Trump says the Iran ceasefire is "over" and threatens a blockade of the Strait of Hormuz. Oil jumped 4.5% Monday. Markets now shift attention to Tuesday's June inflation report, the most important data point of the week, with the Fed watching closely.

What Happened This Week

The truce between the United States and Iran, which had calmed oil markets since mid-June, broke down over the weekend. At the NATO summit, President Trump said the ceasefire "is over" and announced the US would reimpose a blockade on Iranian vessels using the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway between Iran and the Arabian Peninsula that carries roughly a quarter of the world's seaborne oil. Renewed attacks on regional energy infrastructure followed. Crude oil jumped more than 4% on Monday to near $75 a barrel, and Brent, the international benchmark, rose to almost $79. Oil had actually fallen about 8% over the prior month as the ceasefire held, so this reverses a chunk of that relief.

Stocks absorbed the news better than you might expect. The S&P 500, the index of 500 large US companies that most funds and 401(k)s track as "the market," fell on Wednesday when the ceasefire language first turned negative, then recovered to close the week higher, ending Friday at 7,575, a record-ish level, led by Nvidia and Meta. Bond yields told a similar story of uneasy calm: the 10-year Treasury yield, which is the interest rate the government pays to borrow money for ten years and a key benchmark for mortgages and other loans, sits at 4.57%, roughly where it started the week after dipping on Friday when lower oil prices briefly eased inflation worries.

The other major data point was June's jobs report, released July 2. Employers added just 57,000 jobs, about half of the 115,000 economists expected, and April and May were revised down by a combined 74,000. The unemployment rate held at 4.2%, but fewer people were actively looking for work, which is its own warning sign about labor market health.

Strait of Hormuz This narrow shipping lane between Iran and Oman is the route for about a quarter of the world's oil and a similar share of liquefied natural gas exports. Any disruption there tends to push oil prices up quickly, because there is no easy alternate route for that much cargo.

What This Means for Your Portfolio

Your stocks The market shrugged off the ceasefire collapse better than the oil spike alone might suggest, which tells you investors still see this as a regional flare-up rather than a broader economic threat, for now. But a market sitting near record highs, driven heavily by a handful of AI-related names, has less cushion to absorb a real supply shock if this escalates further.
Your bonds and bond funds The 10-year yield is little changed on net this week, but it is a tug of war: weak jobs data pulls yields down because it raises hope of Fed rate cuts, while the oil spike pulls yields up because it raises inflation risk. When yields rise, existing bond prices fall, so this back-and-forth is likely to keep bond fund values choppy rather than trending cleanly in one direction.
Your retirement accounts overall Nothing here demands action today. The bigger question is whether Tuesday's inflation report shows oil's renewed climb feeding through to prices, which would make the Fed's job harder and could keep rates higher for longer. Watching that report matters more this week than watching daily stock moves.

Larry, you are 68 and drawing from your accounts, and Maria's income at 61 means you are not a forced seller in any of this. That matters specifically this week: if the Hormuz situation triggers a sharper selloff, you have the flexibility to wait it out rather than sell into weakness. The Bigger Picture

The US economy is sitting in an uncomfortable spot: growth is visibly slowing, shown by the weak June jobs report, while inflation is still running well above the Federal Reserve's 2% target, at 4.2% in the most recent reading. That combination, slow growth plus high inflation, is called stagflation, and it is the hardest environment for a central bank to manage, because fighting inflation with higher rates risks worsening the job market, while cutting rates to help jobs risks reigniting inflation. The Fed, now led by Chair Kevin Warsh, has leaned hawkish, meaning inclined to keep rates higher rather than cut them, and has actually raised its own inflation forecast for the year.

The Iran conflict adds a second complication on top of that. Even a temporary spike in oil prices can show up in the inflation data a month or two later, which is exactly the kind of thing that could keep the Fed on hold even as the labor market weakens further.

Ceasefire collapses, Hormuz threatened → oil jumps 4.5% → inflation risk rises again → Fed stays cautious on rate cuts, keeping bond yields and mortgage rates elevated

What to Watch Over the Next Two Weeks

  • June CPI report, out Tuesday, July 14. Economists expect the headline number to ease slightly, toward 3.9%, on falling gas prices, but the core rate, which strips out food and energy, is expected to hold near 2.9%, still well above the Fed's target.
  • PPI, the Producer Price Index, which tracks prices businesses pay rather than consumers, follows a day or two after CPI and can hint at where consumer prices are headed next.
  • Whether the Strait of Hormuz situation escalates into an actual shipping disruption, or stays at the level of rhetoric. Insurance costs for tankers transiting the strait are one early signal worth watching.
  • The Fed's July 28-29 meeting. If Tuesday's inflation report comes in hot, expect the Fed to sound even more cautious about cutting rates this year.
  • Whether oil holds above $75. A sustained move above that level would start showing up more clearly in gas prices and, eventually, in inflation data.

Questions to Bring to Your Financial Advisor

1 Given the renewed Iran risk, should our bond duration change?

Duration measures how sensitive a bond or bond fund is to interest rate changes. If you expect more volatility in yields from swinging oil and inflation news, it's worth asking whether your bond holdings are positioned for that, or whether shortening duration makes sense right now.

2 How concentrated is our stock exposure in AI-related names right now?

This week's rally was led narrowly by a few large technology names again. Ask your advisor for a concentration check on your holdings, since a narrow rally can unwind quickly if sentiment shifts, even without new bad economic news.

3 Does this year's inflation path change our Roth conversion plan?

With Larry's RMDs starting in about five years, Maria's income creates room to convert traditional retirement funds to Roth accounts now, potentially at lower tax rates. Ask whether this week's inflation and rate outlook changes the ideal pace or amount for this year's conversion.
