Market Briefing — May 17, 2026

For Larry and Maria  |  Focus: Stocks, Bonds, Retirement Accounts  |  Prepared by Claude
Bond yields hit multi-month highs as inflation surprises to the upside and the Strait of Hormuz stays closed. The 10-year Treasury yield jumped from 4.38% to 4.59% in a single week, and the 30-year crossed above 5%, putting pressure on both stocks and bonds simultaneously. This is the week where the inflation risk from the Middle East conflict started showing up clearly in hard economic data.

What Happened This Week

The biggest market story this week was a sharp jump in bond yields, driven by a hotter-than-expected Producer Price Index reading. The PPI tracks what businesses pay for goods and materials before those costs reach consumers. It jumped 6% over the past 12 months, the largest annual increase since December 2022. That number alarmed investors. The 10-year Treasury yield, which is the interest rate the US government pays to borrow money for 10 years, climbed from 4.38% the week prior to 4.59% by May 15. The 30-year Treasury yield moved above 5%. When bond yields rise like this, the value of existing bonds falls, because older bonds paying lower rates become less attractive than newly issued ones paying more.

The inflation pressure traces directly to the US-Iran conflict, now in its third month. The Strait of Hormuz, a narrow waterway through which roughly 20% of the world's traded oil flows every day, remains effectively closed. Oil prices have surged as a result. West Texas Intermediate crude, the US benchmark, rose roughly 11% in a single week to around $105 to $106 per barrel. Brent crude, the global benchmark, reached about $109 per barrel. Higher oil prices feed into the cost of manufacturing, shipping, heating, and fuel. Those cost increases eventually show up in the inflation numbers the Federal Reserve watches most closely. The Consumer Price Index for April came in at 3.8% annually, up from prior months and well above the Fed's 2% target.

Stock markets reflected this tension. The S&P 500, an index of the 500 largest US companies, had been on a six-week winning streak and touched record highs around 7,400 points on May 9. By May 15 it was pulling back, down about 1.1% on the day. The market had recovered an earlier 9% decline during April, so year-to-date gains remain intact, but the mood shifted this week from cautious optimism to clear concern.

What is the PPI and why does it matter? The Producer Price Index measures what companies pay for raw materials, components, and services before they make their products. Think of it as an upstream inflation signal. When producer prices rise, companies eventually pass those costs on to consumers, which pushes up the Consumer Price Index. A 6% annual PPI reading is a warning that consumer inflation may not fall as fast as hoped, and it gives the Fed reason to consider raising rates.
What is happening at the Federal Reserve? The Fed, which sets the key interest rate for the US economy, held its rate steady for the third consecutive meeting at a range of 3.5% to 3.75%. The vote was unusually divided: 8 members voted to hold, while 4 dissented, the most disagreement since 1992. Some members want to cut rates; others now believe a hike may be needed to fight inflation. Kevin Warsh is being confirmed as the new Fed chair to replace Jerome Powell on May 15, adding a leadership transition to an already uncertain picture. The next Fed meeting is in mid-June 2026.

What This Means for Your Portfolio

Your stocks The S&P 500 gave back some of April's gains mid-week as bond yields rose. When 30-year yields climb above 5%, stocks face two headwinds at once: investors shift money toward bonds, which now offer a safer return, and higher rates squeeze corporate profits by making business borrowing more expensive. The S&P 500 is still up about 8% for 2026 overall, so the year-to-date picture is not terrible. Energy stocks are an exception and have likely gained this week as oil prices surged. If your portfolio includes energy sector exposure, that is working in your favor right now.
Your bonds and bond funds This was a difficult week for bonds. When the 10-year yield jumps from 4.38% to 4.59% in a single week, the value of existing bonds falls in proportion to how many years until they mature. A fund holding long-term bonds, such as 20-year or 30-year Treasuries, could have lost roughly 2% to 3% of its value this week alone. Shorter-term bonds and money market funds were affected much less, because they mature quickly and roll over into the new, higher rates. If you hold short-term bond funds or money market accounts, the yield you earn is actually rising right now.
Your retirement accounts overall The picture is mixed but not alarming. This week put simultaneous pressure on stocks and longer-term bonds, which is the harder scenario for a retirement portfolio. The important offset is that Maria is still working, which means you have income covering household expenses and are not forced to sell anything. You have time to wait this out. The risk worth watching is whether the Fed raises rates later this year, which could trigger another leg down in both stocks and longer-duration bonds before things stabilize.

One point worth raising with your advisor: Larry is 68 and about five years from Required Minimum Distributions, which begin at age 73. RMDs are IRS-mandated annual withdrawals from traditional IRA and 401k accounts. If account balances fall in value before age 73, the required amounts will be smaller and the tax hit lighter. If balances recover and grow, the RMDs will be larger. Either way, the five years before RMDs begin is the planning window for strategies like Roth conversions, which can reduce the tax burden in retirement. This week's volatility is a useful prompt to review that planning with your advisor.

The Bigger Picture

The dominant concern among economists right now is stagflation, a combination of stagnant economic growth and persistent inflation. It is the hardest environment to manage because the Fed's main tool, raising interest rates, helps fight inflation but slows the economy further at the same time. The last major stagflation period in the US was the 1970s, and it took years to work through. Most economists are careful to say the current situation is not that severe, but the parallels are hard to dismiss: an energy price shock from geopolitical disruption, a divided Fed caught between competing pressures, and bond markets demanding higher yields as compensation for uncertainty.

The key variable most analysts are watching is the Strait of Hormuz. If a diplomatic resolution allows the waterway to reopen, oil prices drop quickly, inflation fears ease, bond yields fall, and stocks likely rally sharply. If the conflict extends into summer, sustained high energy prices will continue working their way through the economy, and the window for the Fed to avoid raising rates narrows. The International Energy Agency warned this week that global oil markets could remain severely undersupplied through October even if fighting stops next month.

What to Watch Over the Next Two Weeks

  • June 10: CPI report for May. This is the most important data release in the next few weeks. If May consumer inflation rises further, especially the Core number (which strips out food and energy), it signals inflation is spreading beyond just oil and a Fed rate hike becomes much more likely. If CPI surprises to the downside, markets will rally. Circle this date.
  • June 5: May jobs report. The last report showed 115,000 jobs added in April and an unemployment rate of 4.3%. A weakening job market alongside high inflation confirms stagflation fears. A strong jobs number gives the Fed more confidence to raise rates. Either outcome has significant implications.
  • Mid-June: Federal Reserve meeting. Given the PPI shock and the most divided FOMC vote since 1992, this meeting carries unusual weight. Watch for any shift in language from "monitoring" to "prepared to act." That language shift would signal a rate hike is coming as soon as the July or September meeting.
  • Strait of Hormuz diplomatic developments. Any credible news about ceasefire talks between the US and Iran, or about tanker passage resuming, would be a major positive catalyst. Oil prices respond immediately to these headlines, and a drop in oil directly eases inflation and bond yield pressure.
  • Watch the 10-year yield at 4.75%. If the 10-year Treasury yield pushes past 4.75%, expect another leg down in stock prices and intensifying debate about rate hikes. If it pulls back below 4.4%, the market is signaling that inflation fears are beginning to ease.

Questions to Bring to Your Financial Advisor

1 How is our bond exposure positioned given where yields are now?

When bond yields rise sharply, the funds hit hardest are the long-duration ones, meaning those with more years until they mature. Short-term bonds are relatively unharmed and actually benefit as they roll over at higher rates. Ask your advisor specifically whether your bond holdings lean toward short-term, intermediate-term, or long-term bonds, and whether any rebalancing makes sense now that the 30-year yield is above 5%. Also ask what your money market funds and short-term Treasury holdings are currently yielding compared to six or twelve months ago.

2 Should we be doing any Roth conversions this year?

A Roth conversion means moving money from a traditional IRA (where you pay taxes on withdrawals) to a Roth IRA (where withdrawals are tax-free). You pay income tax on the converted amount the year you do it. Two factors make this worth discussing now. First, if account values have pulled back due to market volatility, converting at lower values means you pay tax on a smaller amount. Second, Larry is about five years from Required Minimum Distributions at age 73. Doing conversions while Maria is still working and your tax situation is predictable may reduce lifetime taxes compared to letting traditional IRA balances grow larger and being forced into larger withdrawals later. Ask your advisor to model projected RMD amounts at current versus projected future balances.

3 How much cash and short-term reserves should we be holding right now?

With the 30-year yield above 5% and the possibility of further volatility, many advisors recommend holding more cash or short-term bonds during uncertain stretches, especially for retirees. This protection works two ways: it keeps you from having to sell stocks at a loss to cover living expenses, and short-term bonds and money market funds are now paying meaningfully higher rates than they were two years ago. Ask whether your current cash reserve is appropriate for your situation, and whether any money sitting in low-yield savings accounts should be moved into higher-yielding short-term alternatives.

4 If the Fed raises rates later this year, what happens to our portfolio?

The Fed has not raised rates yet, but this week's inflation data and the divided vote make it a real possibility in late 2026. Ask your advisor to walk through a concrete scenario: if the Fed raises rates by 0.25% at the June or September meeting, what would the likely near-term impact be on your stock holdings and bond holdings? Knowing the answer in advance helps you avoid emotional decisions in the moment and may surface portfolio adjustments worth making now rather than after the fact.
