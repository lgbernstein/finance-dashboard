Market Briefing — June 22, 2026

For Larry and Maria  |  Focus: Stocks, Bonds, Retirement Accounts  |  Prepared by Claude
Kevin Warsh's first Fed meeting signals a possible rate hike later this year, rattling markets before a partial recovery Nearly half of Fed policymakers now expect at least one rate increase in 2026, a shift that sent stocks down sharply on Wednesday before a rebound through the end of the shortened holiday week.

What Happened This Week

The biggest event of the week was the Federal Reserve's June meeting, the first chaired by Kevin Warsh. The Fed left its key interest rate unchanged at 3.5% to 3.75%, as expected. But the new language that came with the decision was more hawkish, meaning it leaned toward tighter policy, than many investors had hoped for. Nine of the 18 Fed policymakers now expect the Fed to raise rates at least once before the end of 2026, up from essentially none a few months ago. Six of those nine expect two separate rate increases. That is a significant shift in thinking inside the central bank.

What "hawkish" means When economists call the Fed "hawkish," it means the Fed is leaning toward higher interest rates to fight inflation. Higher rates make borrowing more expensive, which slows spending and brings prices down. The opposite, "dovish," means leaning toward lower rates to support growth and jobs.

The market reaction on Wednesday, June 17, was sharp. The S&P 500 (a broad index of 500 large U.S. companies that tracks how the overall stock market is doing) fell 1.21% on the day, the worst single-day drop during a Fed announcement under a new chair since 1994. Tech stocks and growth-oriented shares fell hardest because higher interest rates hurt those companies most. However, markets stabilized and bounced back through Thursday, ahead of the Juneteenth holiday on Friday. For the week overall, the S&P 500 gained about 0.9%, the Dow Jones rose 0.7%, and the Nasdaq (which tracks technology stocks) jumped 2.4%, finishing strongly despite the Fed-day scare.

On the inflation front, the most recent reading, released June 10, showed the Consumer Price Index rose 4.2% over the past year. The CPI is the main government measure of inflation, tracking prices on everyday goods and services. That 4.2% rate is more than double the Fed's 2% target, and it was the hottest reading since 2023. Nearly all of that inflation came from energy costs, which were up 23.5% over the past year due to the US-Iran war earlier this year. The good news: oil prices have fallen sharply. West Texas Intermediate crude, the main U.S. oil benchmark, dropped to about $73.53 a barrel this week, down roughly 22% over the past month as the Iran peace deal continues to take hold. Lower oil now flowing through the system should cool that inflation number noticeably in the months ahead.

Oil's direction this year Oil spiked near $100 a barrel when the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway through which about 20% of the world's oil flows, was blocked during the US-Iran conflict. Since the peace deal was signed in June, that shipping route has begun reopening, and oil has fallen sharply. Cheaper oil means lower gasoline prices, lower shipping costs, and eventually lower prices on many goods. This is the main reason economists expect inflation to cool in coming months.

The 10-year Treasury yield ended the week at 4.49%. The 10-year Treasury yield is the interest rate the U.S. government pays to borrow money for 10 years, and it is the key benchmark that drives mortgage rates, business borrowing costs, and stock valuations. It rose after the Fed's hawkish statement but eased from its peak as markets settled.

What This Means for Your Portfolio

Your stocks The week ended higher despite the Wednesday selloff, which is encouraging. However, the Fed signaling possible rate hikes introduces real uncertainty. Growth and technology stocks are most vulnerable if rates rise further because higher borrowing costs reduce the future value of their earnings. More defensive, dividend-paying stocks, such as utilities and consumer staples, tend to hold up better in a rising-rate environment. Ask your advisor if your stock mix reflects the possibility of one or two rate hikes this year.
Your bonds and bond funds Bond prices move in the opposite direction of interest rates: when rates rise, existing bond values fall. With the Fed now signaling possible hikes, bond prices face some downward pressure. However, rising rates also mean new bonds and CDs pay higher interest. Short-term Treasuries and money market funds are currently paying around 4% to 4.5%, which is attractive by historical standards. If you own longer-term bond funds, check with your advisor about whether your duration, meaning how long until those bonds mature, is appropriate given this interest-rate outlook.
Your retirement accounts overall The big picture remains manageable. Maria's income means you are not forced to sell anything. The stock market, despite the Fed-day drop, recovered within days and finished the week higher. Oil's continued decline is easing the inflation that has been the dominant concern for months. If the Fed does raise rates once or twice, that will be a headwind, but not a crisis. The key risk to watch is whether inflation stays stubborn, forcing more Fed action than markets currently expect.

One item specific to your situation: Larry's RMDs (required minimum distributions, the mandatory annual withdrawals the IRS requires from traditional IRAs and 401k accounts starting at age 73) are about five years away. The period between now and then is your best window for Roth conversions, moving money from a tax-deferred account to a tax-free Roth IRA. If the Fed does raise rates and stocks pull back modestly, that creates a lower-cost moment to convert. Worth discussing with your advisor now.

The Bigger Picture

The dominant story in markets right now is the transition from a war-driven inflation shock toward a new normal. Earlier this year, the US-Iran conflict spiked oil to near $100, pushed inflation to 4.2%, and created genuine fear of a 1970s-style stagflation scenario. Stagflation is when you have high inflation and weak economic growth at the same time, which is particularly bad because the usual tools to fight each problem make the other one worse. That extreme fear has eased significantly. Oil is falling, the peace deal is holding, and the labor market remains solid with 172,000 jobs added in May and unemployment at 4.3%.

But the Fed is not out of the woods. Inflation at 4.2% gives the new chair Kevin Warsh very little room to maneuver. He is signaling he will not ignore high inflation even if it is mostly oil-driven. That is probably the right call for credibility, but it means the risk of one or two rate increases is real for the rest of the year. Markets are adjusting to that reality. The economy is still growing, jobs are plentiful, and the geopolitical crisis is winding down. For a retired investor with Maria's income as a buffer, this is a time to stay positioned and be patient rather than to make dramatic changes.

Fed signals possible rate hike(s) in 2026 → bond yields rise, making borrowing more expensive → stock valuations face pressure, especially growth/tech shares → but oil falling toward $70s quietly fights inflation from the other direction, potentially reducing the number of hikes needed

What to Watch Over the Next Two Weeks

  • June CPI report, due July 14. This will be the first inflation reading that fully captures lower oil prices after the Iran deal. If the annual rate drops from 4.2% toward 3.5% or below, it reduces the pressure on the Fed to hike rates. That is the single most important number for your portfolio right now.
  • June jobs report, due July 2. The economy added 172,000 jobs in May. If June shows similar strength, the Fed gains more confidence the economy can handle higher rates without tipping into recession. A surprisingly weak number would complicate the picture.
  • Iran nuclear talks progress. The 60-day negotiations that began with the June peace deal are ongoing. Any sign those talks are collapsing could send oil back toward $90-$100, reigniting inflation fears and market volatility.
  • The 10-year Treasury yield. Watch the 4.25% level on the low side and the 4.75% level on the high side. A move below 4.25% would signal markets believe inflation is cooling faster than expected, which is good for bonds and stocks. A move above 4.75% would signal renewed inflation alarm.
  • Mortgage rates. Currently averaging 6.53% on a 30-year fixed loan. These are unlikely to fall meaningfully until the Fed signals its rate-hike cycle is truly over. Housing affordability remains severely strained for buyers, which weighs on the broader economy.

Questions to Bring to Your Financial Advisor

1 Given that the Fed may raise rates once or twice this year, should we adjust the bond portion of our portfolio?

When interest rates rise, bonds lose value, especially longer-term bonds. The key number is "duration," which measures how sensitive a bond fund is to rate changes. A fund with a 10-year duration drops about 10% in value if rates rise by 1%. Ask your advisor what your current duration is, and whether shorter-duration bonds or floating-rate instruments might reduce your risk given the new Fed outlook.

2 Is this a good moment to do a Roth conversion, and how much should we consider converting this year?

Larry's RMDs begin at age 73, roughly five years away. Converting traditional IRA or 401k money to a Roth now means paying taxes today, but everything in the Roth grows tax-free and is never subject to mandatory withdrawals. Maria's income helps cover the tax bill on any conversion. If the stock market pulls back modestly due to rate-hike fears, converting at lower prices is more efficient. Ask your advisor for a target amount to convert each year between now and 2031 to stay in a favorable tax bracket.

3 How much of our stock portfolio is in rate-sensitive growth and tech stocks versus more defensive holdings?

The Fed's rate-hike signal hit tech and growth stocks hardest on Wednesday. These stocks carry high valuations partly because investors assume interest rates will stay low. If rates rise, that assumption changes and those stocks can fall further than the broader market. Defensive sectors like utilities, consumer staples (food and household products), and healthcare tend to hold up better. Ask your advisor how your stock allocation breaks down and whether any rebalancing makes sense.

4 With oil falling and inflation possibly cooling, when might the Fed shift back to talking about rate cuts?

This is the big forward-looking question. If the June and July CPI reports show inflation dropping toward 3% or below, the case for rate hikes weakens considerably. A Fed that does not hike, or that reverses course sooner than expected, is good for bonds and for growth stocks. Ask your advisor when they think the Fed might pivot back toward neutral or lower rates, and how your portfolio is positioned for that scenario versus the scenario where hikes actually happen.
