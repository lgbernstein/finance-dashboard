Market Briefing — May 25, 2026

For Larry and Maria  |  Focus: Stocks, Bonds, Retirement Accounts  |  Prepared by Claude
US-Iran peace deal hopes push oil sharply lower, giving stocks their 8th straight weekly gain. Both sides are now discussing a formal agreement to end hostilities and reopen the Strait of Hormuz. Oil fell 5% this week to about $91 a barrel. The deal is still not final, and the situation could reverse quickly, but this is the most positive geopolitical development since the conflict began.

What Happened This Week

The biggest story this week was progress on US-Iran peace negotiations. Both countries are now discussing a "memorandum of understanding," which is essentially a written framework for ending the conflict and setting out a path to resolve all remaining disputes. Markets reacted immediately. Oil prices fell more than 5% on Monday alone, dropping from about $96 a barrel to $91. That drop matters because oil prices have been the main driver of inflation since the conflict began in early May.

Why oil prices affect almost everything you buy Oil is an input into nearly every product and service. When oil costs more, shipping costs more, manufacturing costs more, and food costs more. The CPI (Consumer Price Index), which is the government's main measure of how fast prices are rising, jumped to 3.8% annually in April, with energy accounting for more than 40% of that monthly increase. Any deal that lowers oil prices puts direct downward pressure on inflation.

US stocks extended their winning streak to eight consecutive weeks, the longest run since December 2023. The S&P 500 (an index that tracks the 500 largest US companies and serves as the main benchmark for the stock market) gained about 0.4% this week. The Dow Jones Industrial Average hit an intraday record high on Friday. Corporate earnings have been strong: 84% of S&P 500 companies that have reported quarterly results beat analysts' expectations, well above the five-year average of 78%.

Bond yields (the interest rate the government pays to borrow money, and the benchmark for mortgage rates and many other loans) remained elevated but steady. The 10-year Treasury yield hovered around 4.57% to 4.62% this week. When yields are this high, bond prices are lower than they were a few years ago. On the Federal Reserve front, Kevin Warsh was sworn in as the new Fed Chair this week. The Fed held interest rates steady at its last meeting with a 3.50% to 3.75% target range. Bank of America now expects the Fed will not cut rates until July or September 2027, meaning rates stay higher for longer than many investors had hoped.

What This Means for Your Portfolio

Your stocks This was a good week. The market's 8th straight weekly gain, strong earnings reports, and falling oil prices all point in the right direction. If an Iran deal is finalized, expect a larger rally. The risk is that the deal falls apart or talks stall, which could push oil back above $100 and reverse some of these gains quickly.
Your bonds and bond funds Yields remain elevated, which means the bonds you already hold are worth less than they were before yields rose. The 10-year Treasury at 4.57% and the 30-year at about 5.12% are at levels not seen in many years. New money going into bonds now earns a better rate than at any point in the last decade. If the Fed eventually cuts rates, bond prices will rise and recover some of that lost value. For now, hold steady and collect the income.
Your retirement accounts overall The combination of an 8-week stock rally and the largest S&P earnings beat rate in years means account balances are likely higher than they were a few months ago. The key watch item is whether the Iran situation resolves. If it does, inflation cools, the Fed has more room to cut rates, and both stocks and bonds benefit. If it does not, the environment stays difficult. The good news is that you are not in a position where you have to sell anything.

Maria's income is a real advantage right now. You are not forced to liquidate positions at any particular price. Larry, at 68, you have roughly five years before Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) begin. RMDs are mandatory annual withdrawals from traditional IRAs and 401(k) accounts that the IRS requires starting at age 73. The five-year window before they start is the best time to review whether converting some of those funds to Roth accounts makes sense, because Roth accounts have no RMDs and grow tax-free. This is worth a specific conversation with your advisor.

The Bigger Picture

The US economy is in a complicated spot. On the surface, things look decent: stocks are up, unemployment is 4.3% (historically low), and corporate profits are strong. But underneath, inflation at 3.8% is well above the Fed's 2% target, and the cause (energy disruption from a war) is something the Fed cannot fix by raising rates. The Fed's traditional tool for fighting inflation is to raise borrowing costs and slow spending. That works when inflation is caused by too much demand. It does not work as well when inflation is caused by a blocked oil strait. This is why there is growing concern about stagflation, which is the combination of high inflation and slow economic growth. It is the hardest environment for a retirement portfolio because both stocks and bonds struggle at the same time.

The Iran peace deal, if it happens, changes that picture significantly. Economists expect oil to fall toward $70 to $80 a barrel if the Strait reopens and normal shipping resumes. That would pull inflation back toward the Fed's target, give the Fed room to cut rates, and likely produce a broad market rally. The path forward depends heavily on the next few weeks of diplomatic progress.

US-Iran deal progress → Strait of Hormuz talks advance → oil falls toward $85-91 → energy inflation eases → overall CPI falls toward 3% → Fed gains room to cut rates → bond prices recover, mortgage rates fall → stocks rally further on easier financial conditions

What to Watch Over the Next Two Weeks

  • US-Iran deal news (ongoing): This is the single biggest variable. A signed memorandum of understanding would likely trigger an immediate oil drop and stock rally. A collapse in talks would reverse this week's gains. Watch for any statements from the State Department or Iranian foreign ministry.
  • May jobs report (Friday, June 5): The government releases its monthly jobs count and unemployment rate on the first Friday of each month. April showed only 115,000 new jobs, which is below the 150,000-200,000 pace considered healthy. A second weak jobs report would raise recession concerns. A strong report would calm those fears but might also make the Fed less likely to cut rates.
  • June CPI report (Wednesday, June 10): The Consumer Price Index for May will be released. If oil's drop this week shows up in the data, this could be a positive surprise. The headline number is expected to come in lower than April's 3.8%, though it will still be above the Fed's 2% target.
  • New Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's first public statements: Warsh was sworn in this week. Investors will closely watch his first speeches for signals about whether he will push for rate cuts sooner than his predecessor planned, or maintain the current higher-for-longer stance. His first major public appearance will set the tone for markets.
  • 10-year Treasury yield threshold: If the 10-year yield drops below 4.25%, that would signal that bond investors believe inflation is coming under control and rate cuts are coming. If it rises above 4.75%, that signals renewed inflation fears and continued pressure on stock valuations.

Questions to Bring to Your Financial Advisor

1 Should we do any Roth conversions this year while the five-year window before RMDs is still open?

Larry turns 68 this year, meaning Required Minimum Distributions start in about five years (age 73). A Roth conversion means moving money from a traditional IRA (where withdrawals are taxed) into a Roth IRA (where withdrawals are tax-free). The converted amount gets taxed now, but everything it earns from that point on is never taxed again, and you never have to take RMDs from a Roth account.

This year may be a good time to evaluate this because the stock market is up (conversion amounts are based on current account values), and Maria is still working which means the household has income to potentially offset the tax hit. Ask your advisor: "Given our current income, account balances, and the RMD clock, how much should we consider converting each year between now and age 73?"

2 How should we position our bond holdings given that rates may stay elevated through 2027?

Bank of America and several other major banks now believe the Fed will not cut interest rates until mid-to-late 2027. That means bond prices, which fall when rates rise and recover when rates fall, may stay depressed for another year or more. The question is whether to stay the course, shift toward shorter-duration bonds (which are less sensitive to rate changes), or take advantage of current high yields by locking in rates now.

Ask your advisor: "Given that rate cuts may be 18 months away, should we be shifting our bond allocation toward shorter maturities or individual Treasury bonds rather than bond funds?"

3 With stocks up eight weeks in a row, should we rebalance our portfolio?

When stocks outperform bonds over several months, your portfolio can drift from its target allocation. For example, if you started the year with 60% stocks and 40% bonds, and stocks are up while bonds are flat, you might now have 65% stocks and 35% bonds. Rebalancing means selling some of the winner (stocks) and buying more of the laggard (bonds) to return to your target.

After eight consecutive up weeks, this is a good time to check. Ask your advisor: "Has our portfolio drifted meaningfully from our target allocation, and if so, is this a good time to rebalance given the Iran situation?"

4 What happens to our income from bonds and money market funds if the Fed eventually cuts rates?

Right now, high-quality short-term bonds and money market funds (savings-like accounts that invest in short-term government debt) are paying roughly 4.5% to 5%. That income will drop when the Fed cuts rates. Money market fund rates fall almost immediately after a Fed cut. Individual bonds locked in before the cut keep paying their original rate until they mature.

Ask your advisor: "How much of our income currently depends on rates staying this high, and what is our plan to replace that income if the Fed cuts rates in 2027?"
