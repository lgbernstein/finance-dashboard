Market Briefing — June 13, 2026

For Larry and Maria  |  Focus: Stocks, Bonds, Retirement Accounts  |  Prepared by Claude
A US-Iran peace deal looks close. Oil fell, stocks finished a strong week, but inflation is still high at 4.2%. The week brought real relief. The risk now is that high inflation keeps the Federal Reserve from cutting interest rates, even as the war winds down.

What Happened This Week

The big story was progress toward ending the US-Iran war. On Thursday, President Trump said he expects to sign a deal soon to end the conflict, and on Friday Pakistan's prime minister said the two sides had agreed on the wording of a deal. Markets liked the news. Oil prices fell about 4% on the week, with US crude dropping to roughly $86 a barrel from levels near $100 earlier in the conflict.

Lower oil pulled bond yields down. The 10-year Treasury yield fell to 4.49% on Friday, down from 4.55% a week earlier. A Treasury yield is the interest rate the US government pays to borrow money. The 10-year is the most watched one because it sets the tone for mortgages, business loans, and how investors value stocks. When the yield falls, it usually means investors expect calmer inflation ahead.

Stocks finished the week near record highs. The S&P 500, an index of 500 large US companies and a good stand-in for the overall market, is up about 9% so far in 2026. The mood was helped by a strong stock market debut from SpaceX, which jumped almost 20% on its first day of trading. The one sour note came earlier in the week. The May inflation report showed prices rose 4.2% over the past year, the hottest reading since 2023, driven almost entirely by high energy costs from the war.

What does 4.2% inflation mean? Inflation is the rate at which prices rise. A 4.2% reading means a basket of goods that cost $100 a year ago now costs $104.20. The Federal Reserve wants this number near 2%. Most of the current overshoot comes from energy, which was up 23.5% over the past year because of the war. Food and housing costs have been far calmer.

What This Means for Your Portfolio

Your stocks A good week. Stocks rose as the war looked closer to ending, and the S&P 500 sits near record highs, up about 9% this year. The peace progress removed some of the fear that had hung over the market. The thing to keep in mind is that stocks are priced for good news, so any breakdown in the Iran talks could bring a quick pullback.
Your bonds and bond funds Also a good week. Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions. When yields fall, the bonds you already own rise in value. Yields fell this week, so your existing bonds and bond funds gained a little. New money put into safe short-term Treasuries or money market funds still earns around 4% to 4.5%, the best safe return in years.
Your retirement accounts overall Holding steady to slightly better. Both stocks and bonds gained this week, so a typical retirement mix moved up. Nothing here calls for action. The high inflation reading is worth watching, but it did not derail the week.

Your situation gives you room to stay calm. Larry is 68 and retired, drawing income from your accounts. Maria is 61 and still working, which means household income is coming in and you are not forced to sell investments at a bad time to pay bills. Required Minimum Distributions, the withdrawals the IRS forces from traditional retirement accounts, begin at age 73, roughly five years away for Larry. That gives you a planning window now, while markets are strong, to consider moves like Roth conversions before those forced withdrawals start.

The Bigger Picture

The economy is caught between two forces. On one side, the US-Iran war pushed oil and energy prices up sharply, and that single factor is keeping headline inflation high at 4.2%. On the other side, the rest of the economy looks steady. The May jobs report showed employers added 172,000 jobs, more than double what economists expected, and the unemployment rate held at 4.3%. A steady job market plus high inflation leaves the Federal Reserve stuck. It does not want to cut interest rates while inflation runs hot, but the inflation is coming from an oil shock that rate cuts cannot fix.

This is why the peace news matters so much. If the war ends and oil keeps falling, inflation should cool on its own over the coming months. That would free the Fed to consider rate cuts later in the year and take pressure off mortgages and bonds. If the talks collapse and oil spikes again, the opposite happens. The whole market narrative right now hinges on whether the Iran deal holds.

What to Watch Over the Next Two Weeks

  • The Federal Reserve meeting on June 16 and 17. This is the first meeting led by new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh. No rate change is expected, but his comments will signal whether rate cuts are possible later this year.
  • Whether the US-Iran deal actually gets signed. A signed deal would likely push oil lower and help both stocks and bonds. A breakdown would do the reverse.
  • Oil prices. US crude near $86 is the key number. A drop toward $80 means inflation relief is coming. A jump back toward $100 means more inflation pressure.
  • The 10-year Treasury yield, now at 4.49%. If it falls below 4.25%, investors believe inflation is cooling. If it climbs back above 4.75%, they are worried again.
  • The next inflation report, covering June, is due July 14. It will show whether the energy spike is starting to fade from the numbers.

Questions to Bring to Your Financial Advisor

1 With stocks near record highs, should we take some gains or rebalance?

The S&P 500 is up about 9% this year and sitting near highs. Because Larry is retired and drawing from the accounts, it can make sense to trim some stock gains into safer holdings, so a sudden drop does not force selling at low prices. Ask whether your current mix of stocks and bonds still matches your comfort level and income needs.

2 Does high inflation change how we should hold our bonds?

Inflation at 4.2% eats into the fixed payments that bonds make. Short-term Treasuries and money market funds now pay around 4% to 4.5% with little risk. Ask whether it makes sense to favor shorter-term bonds for now, which are less hurt if interest rates stay high, and to use today's high safe yields for the cash portion of your accounts.

3 Is this a good year for a Roth conversion?

You have a roughly five-year window before Larry's Required Minimum Distributions begin at 73. A Roth conversion moves money from a traditional IRA, which is taxed when you withdraw, into a Roth IRA, which grows and pays out tax-free and never has forced withdrawals. Maria's income can help cover the tax bill. Ask how much to convert each year and which tax bracket to stay within.

4 How much cash cushion should we keep given the war and oil swings?

The Iran situation can move markets fast in either direction. A cash buffer covering one to two years of withdrawals means you never have to sell stocks during a sharp drop. Ask whether your current cash reserve is the right size for this kind of headline-driven market.
