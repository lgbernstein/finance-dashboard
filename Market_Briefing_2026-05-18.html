Market Briefing — May 18, 2026

For Larry and Maria  |  Focus: Stocks, Bonds, Retirement Accounts  |  Prepared by Claude
Bond Yields Hit 15-Month High as Inflation Jumps to 3.8% on Iran War Energy Shock The 10-year Treasury yield touched 4.59% this week, its highest level since February 2025. The driver is April inflation data that came in far above expectations, fueled almost entirely by an energy price surge from the ongoing U.S.-Iran conflict. Higher yields mean lower bond values and more pressure on stocks.

What Happened This Week

The dominant story this week is inflation, and specifically where it is coming from. The government's Consumer Price Index report for April (released May 12) showed prices rising 3.8% compared to a year ago. That is the fastest rate since May 2023, and it caught many investors off guard. The main culprit is energy. Gasoline prices are up 28% from a year ago and fuel oil is up 54%. Both are direct results of the U.S.-Iran war and the disruption to the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway through which roughly 20% of the world's traded oil normally flows. When that waterway is threatened or partially blocked, the entire global oil market scrambles for supply, and prices rise sharply everywhere.

Consumer Price Index (CPI) — what it means The CPI is a monthly report from the government measuring how much prices have changed for a standard basket of goods: groceries, gas, housing, clothing, medical care, and so on. An annual CPI of 3.8% means that on average, things cost 3.8% more than they did a year ago. The Federal Reserve's target is 2%. Anything above that puts pressure on the Fed to keep interest rates high — or even raise them.

The bond market reacted immediately. The yield on the 10-year U.S. Treasury note, which is the interest rate the government pays to borrow money for 10 years, rose to 4.59%. To put that in context, this is the highest it has been in about 15 months. Meanwhile, the 30-year Treasury yield is at 5.12%, a level that signals bond investors are demanding significantly more compensation for the risk that inflation will eat into their returns over time. When yields rise, the value of bonds that are already held in your portfolio falls — because those older bonds are now paying less than what new bonds offer.

Why rising yields hurt existing bond owners Imagine you own a bond that pays 3% per year. Now new bonds are paying 5%. Nobody will pay full price for your 3% bond when they can buy a 5% bond instead. To sell your bond, you would have to accept a lower price. This is not a realized loss unless you sell, but the market value of your bond has gone down. Bond funds, which many retirement accounts hold, reflect this daily in their share prices.

In stocks, the week has been mixed but unsettled. The S&P 500, which is an index tracking the 500 largest U.S. companies, hit record highs earlier in May driven by gains in technology stocks. But on Friday May 15, stocks dropped 1.2% as the inflation data and rising yields rattled investors. As of Monday morning May 18, markets are starting the week in negative territory again, with the Iran conflict still uncertain. Major earnings reports from Nvidia (a leading artificial intelligence chip company) and Walmart are due this week, and those results will either calm or further rattle investor confidence.

On the geopolitical front, the situation in the Middle East remains fluid. On May 6, President Trump paused "Operation Project Freedom" — a U.S. Navy mission escorting merchant ships out of the Persian Gulf — citing "great progress" toward a possible agreement with Iran. That pause has not yet produced a deal, and the Strait of Hormuz remains only partially open to commercial shipping. Insurance costs for ships transiting the region have surged dramatically, and shipping volume through the route is down more than 90% from pre-war levels. Until the oil flow normalizes, energy prices — and with them inflation — are unlikely to fall quickly.

What This Means for Your Portfolio

Your stocks U.S. stocks have given back some of their recent gains under pressure from rising bond yields and inflation fears. Technology stocks led the earlier rally, and those same stocks are now most vulnerable to higher yields — because tech companies are often valued on expected future profits, and higher rates make future money worth less today. Energy stocks (oil companies) are one notable exception: they tend to benefit when oil prices are high. If you hold broad index funds or a mix of sectors, the overall picture has softened but has not been a disaster. Nvidia's earnings Wednesday will be a key test of whether AI-driven tech enthusiasm remains intact.
Your bonds and bond funds This is the more difficult area right now. Rising yields mean falling bond prices, and the moves this week have been significant. The 10-year yield at 4.59% and the 30-year at 5.12% reflect serious concern in the bond market that inflation will stay elevated longer than hoped. If you hold intermediate or long-term bond funds in your retirement accounts, those funds have likely declined in value in recent weeks. Short-term bonds and money market funds (accounts that hold very short-term government debt) are much less affected and are actually paying higher interest as a result of today's rate environment — something worth discussing with your advisor.
Your retirement accounts overall The big picture is a portfolio under mild to moderate stress. Neither stocks nor bonds have had a catastrophic week, but the direction of travel — higher inflation, higher yields, geopolitical uncertainty — is not favorable in the short term. The most important thing to know: you and Maria are not in a forced-selling situation. Maria's income covers living expenses, which means you do not have to sell assets at currently depressed prices to fund your life. That is a genuine advantage. Your RMDs (Required Minimum Distributions, the annual withdrawals the IRS mandates from traditional retirement accounts) do not begin until you turn 73, which is roughly five years away. You have time to let this environment normalize before those distributions are required.

One specific dynamic worth noting: inflation at 3.8% erodes purchasing power. If your retirement account earns 5% but inflation is 3.8%, your real (after-inflation) gain is only about 1.2%. This makes the inflation environment directly relevant to retirement planning — not just as an abstract economic concern but as something that affects how far your money goes each month. The five-year window before RMDs is also your window to consider Roth conversion strategies — moving money from traditional IRA accounts (where you pay tax when you withdraw) to Roth accounts (where growth and withdrawals are tax-free). This is worth modeling with your advisor while tax rates and your current income bracket may favor it.

The Bigger Picture

The U.S. economy is navigating a second inflationary episode in four years, but this one has a different cause than the first. The 2021-2023 inflation surge came from COVID-related supply-chain problems and the burst of spending that followed lockdowns. The Federal Reserve raised interest rates from near zero to over 5% to cool that inflation, and it worked — CPI came down from 9% to around 3%. Then the Fed started cautiously cutting rates in late 2025. Now, a new shock has arrived in the form of an energy price surge driven by war in the Middle East. This type of inflation is harder for the Fed to address, because raising interest rates cannot reopen the Strait of Hormuz or lower oil prices directly. The Fed is in an uncomfortable position: inflation is above target, but the economy is also showing signs of slowing (April added only 115,000 jobs, well below a healthy pace of 150,000-200,000). Some economists call this combination "stagflation" — rising prices alongside slowing growth — and it is one of the most challenging environments for investors.

The Federal Reserve held rates steady at 3.5-3.75% at its April meeting, but the decision was unusually fractious. Four of the twelve voting members dissented, which has not happened since 1992. Some wanted to cut rates to support the economy; others objected to language that even hinted at future cuts, arguing inflation makes that premature. The Fed's next scheduled meeting is in June. Markets are not pricing in any rate change at that meeting. Bank of America's economists now say they do not expect the Fed to cut rates again until July 2027. That is a meaningful statement: it means higher borrowing costs are likely to persist for more than a year.

Iran war disrupts Strait of Hormuz → oil supply restricted → energy prices surge (gasoline up 28%) → CPI jumps to 3.8% → Fed cannot cut rates → bond yields rise to 15-month highs → bond values fall, stocks under pressure → a deal reopening the Strait would reverse this chain quickly

What to Watch Over the Next Two Weeks

  • Iran diplomatic negotiations (ongoing): Trump paused Operation Project Freedom on May 6, citing progress. Any confirmed deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz would be the single most market-moving event possible right now — it would push oil prices down, ease inflation fears, lower bond yields, and likely trigger a stock rally. Watch for news from the State Department or White House about Iran talks.
  • Nvidia earnings — Wednesday May 20: Nvidia is the bellwether for artificial intelligence investment. If it beats earnings expectations and raises its outlook, it signals the AI spending boom is intact and will likely lift tech stocks broadly. If it disappoints, expect further selling in the sector.
  • Walmart earnings — Thursday May 21: Walmart serves as a window into consumer health. Its results will show whether American households are holding up under inflation pressure or starting to pull back on spending. Reduced spending signals a slowing economy.
  • Weekly jobless claims — Thursday May 22: These weekly numbers show how many Americans filed for unemployment benefits. A rising trend would confirm the labor market is weakening and would add to concerns about the economy slowing even as inflation stays high.
  • Fed communications: Watch for speeches from any Federal Reserve officials between now and the June meeting. The internal dissent at the April meeting means the Fed's direction is genuinely uncertain. Any hint of rate hike language from individual officials would push yields higher and weigh on both stocks and bonds.
  • 10-year yield threshold — 4.75%: If the 10-year Treasury yield crosses 4.75%, it would signal the bond market is pricing in a meaningful risk of a rate hike and would likely accelerate selling in both stocks and bonds. That level is worth tracking on your dashboard.

Questions to Bring to Your Financial Advisor

1 How exposed are we to long-term bonds right now, and should we shorten our duration?

"Duration" is a measure of how sensitive a bond or bond fund is to interest rate changes. Long-duration bonds (those that mature in 10-30 years) lose the most value when yields rise. Short-duration bonds (maturing in 1-3 years) are much less sensitive. Given that yields have risen to 15-month highs and may go higher, it is worth asking whether your bond allocation is positioned for this environment — or whether it was set up for a world where rates were expected to fall.

With the 10-year at 4.59% and the 30-year at 5.12%, short-term Treasuries and money market funds now offer meaningful returns with far less interest-rate risk. Ask your advisor: "What is the average duration of our bond holdings, and does that still make sense?"

2 Should we be doing Roth conversions this year, and how much?

A Roth conversion means moving money from a traditional IRA or 401(k) into a Roth IRA. You pay income tax on the amount converted now, but the Roth account grows tax-free and has no Required Minimum Distributions. The five years before Larry turns 73 are the prime window for this strategy.

The key question is whether converting now — while your taxable income may be lower than it will be when RMDs kick in — saves taxes in the long run. Inflation at 3.8% also makes this relevant: a Roth account that grows tax-free is a better inflation hedge than a traditional account where future withdrawals will be taxed. Ask your advisor: "Have we modeled our projected RMD amounts at age 73, and does a Roth conversion strategy make sense given where we are today?"

3 If the Iran conflict drags on through the summer, what does that do to our plan?

The Strait of Hormuz situation is the central variable in the current economic environment. If a diplomatic deal is reached and oil flows normalize, inflation could ease significantly by fall, and the investment climate would improve. If the conflict drags on, high energy prices and high inflation could persist — and the Fed's ability to help the economy would remain constrained.

Ask your advisor: "Have you stress-tested our plan against a prolonged energy shock scenario where inflation stays above 3.5% through 2027? And are we positioned to withstand that without needing to sell assets at depressed prices?" Since Maria is still working and you are not forced sellers, you may be better positioned than most retirees — but it is worth confirming.

4 Are there any inflation-protective investments we should consider adding?

Certain types of investments are specifically designed to hold their value when inflation is high. TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities) are government bonds whose principal increases with inflation — so the interest you earn rises with prices. I-Bonds are a type of savings bond with returns tied directly to the CPI. Energy sector stocks can also benefit from high oil prices. Commodities like gold sometimes serve as an inflation hedge as well.

Ask your advisor: "Given that inflation is now at 3.8% and may stay elevated, do we have adequate inflation protection in the portfolio? Should we add TIPS or I-Bonds, and what percentage makes sense for our situation?"
